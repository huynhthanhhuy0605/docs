Filling text with an image — a photo showing through the shape of each letter — is a classic Photoshop trick that uses a clipping mask on a type layer. It works, but it means opening Photoshop, keeping the type layer editable, and re-exporting every time you change a word. If you don't own Photoshop or just want it done in a minute, you can get the same photo-in-text look in Canva with the [Text to Frames](/text-to-frames/what-is-text-to-frames) app — no clipping mask, no flattening, and the letters stay editable.

## The Photoshop way (and why you might skip it)

In Photoshop, you type your word on a type layer, place the photo on the layer directly above it, then create a **clipping mask** (Layer → Create Clipping Mask, or Alt/Option-click between the two layers). The image then shows only inside the letters. It's a solid technique, but it comes with friction: you need a paid Photoshop license, the file has to stay layered to remain editable, and there's no live preview of the final crop until you nudge the photo into place. For a one-off banner or a title you'll tweak a few times, that's a lot of overhead.

## The no-Photoshop way: turn letters into Canva frames

Text to Frames converts each letter of your word into a **real Canva frame** — the same kind of placeholder Canva uses for profile pictures and rounded photos. You drop an image into the letters and it crops to the exact letter shapes. Because these are genuine frames, you can drag to reposition the photo inside the text, zoom it, or swap it out anytime, just like any other Canva frame.

<Note>
This isn't an image pasted on top of text or a PNG overlay. Each letter becomes an editable frame, so the crop follows the letter outlines precisely and stays adjustable after you add it.
</Note>

## Fill text with an image in Canva, step by step

<Steps>
<Step title="Open Text to Frames in Canva">
Open a design (a Presentation, Poster, or Instagram Post works well), go to the **Apps** panel in the side bar, and open **Text to Frames**. A few Canva design types don't allow frames — if you see *"Frames can't be added to this design type,"* just open the app inside a different design.
</Step>

<Step title="Type your word and pick a font">
Enter your text (up to 200 characters) and choose a font. The free tier includes five varied fonts — Roboto, Open Sans, Lato, Playfair Display, and Pacifico — while Pro unlocks the full library of 100+ fonts. Bold, rounded letters hold a photo best. Use the **B** and **I** buttons for bold or italic; if one is greyed out, the current font doesn't include that style.
</Step>

<Step title="Adjust the letters">
Fine-tune **Letter spacing** (−100 to 100) and **Font size** so the word fills the space cleanly. Turn on **Split into letters** if you want each letter to be its own frame — that lets you drop a *different* photo into every letter.
</Step>

<Step title="Check the live preview and add to design">
The **Preview** at the top updates about half a second after each change, and what you see there is exactly what gets added. When it looks right, click **Add to design**. The letter frames drop onto your page and auto-fit your layout.
</Step>

<Step title="Drop your image into the text">
Drag any photo (or even a video) onto the letter frames — the image fills the shape of the text. Then drag or zoom the photo *inside* the letters to frame it. If you used Split into letters, add a separate image to each one.
</Step>
</Steps>

## Add a border or glow so the text stands out

When a busy photo shows through the letters, the word can get lost against the background. Text to Frames includes three effects (Pro), each with its own color, that stack together:

Use **Border** (0–50) to draw a crisp outline hugging every letter so the text reads clearly over the image. Add **Blur** (0–20) for a soft glow or drop-shadow halo behind the word, giving it a neon or lifted feel. And apply **Inner shadow** (0–40) to add depth inside the letters, so the type looks carved or three-dimensional rather than flat. Free users can still add plain photo-filled text; turning on a Pro effect or Pro font switches the main button to the upgrade path.

## Photoshop vs Canva for photo-filled text

| | Photoshop (clipping mask) | Canva (Text to Frames) |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | Paid subscription | Free tier; Pro for extra fonts and effects |
| Live preview | No — nudge to see the crop | Yes — preview matches the result |
| Editable text | Keep the file layered | Frames stay editable in Canva |
| Different image per letter | Manual, layer by layer | One toggle (Split into letters) |
| Borders / glow / depth | Layer styles | Built-in Border, Blur, Inner shadow |
| Best for | Print-grade, complex compositing | Fast social, banners, titles |

Photoshop still wins for heavy print work and precise compositing. But for a title, a banner, or a social graphic you want to publish today, the Canva route gets you the same photo-in-text effect without a clipping mask — and keeps the words editable.
